Native American Heritage Month lesson: learn about Native nations today in 30 minutes

A free, no prep Native American Heritage Month lesson for grades 3 to 8 on Native nations today: a present tense true or false, with Smithsonian sources.

Grades
3 to 8, with changes for younger and older
Time
30 minutes
Prep
None. Project this page or read from it.
You need
Paper and pencil for each student

What it is

November is National American Indian Heritage Month, also called Native American Heritage Month. The Library of Congress legal research guide traces the observance to 1986, when Congress asked the President to proclaim an American Indian Week. The week moved around the calendar, and in 1990 Congress approved a joint resolution designating November 1990 as National American Indian Heritage Month. Congress chose November because it concluded the traditional harvest season and was a time of thanksgiving and celebration for American Indians. Similar proclamations, under variant names, have been issued each year since 1994.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian (NMAI) runs Native Knowledge 360, a national education initiative built with Native communities. Its framework says that there is no single American Indian culture or language, and that in the Americas there is vast cultural diversity among more than 2,000 tribal groups. It invites teachers to move past "the same old lessons about ancient American Indian food, clothing, and shelter."

The United States recognizes more than 570 American Indian tribes and Alaska Native entities: USA.gov lists 574 and the Bureau of Indian Affairs counted 575 in January 2026. The NMAI framework explains that tribal governments operate under self-chosen traditional or constitution-based structures as sovereign nations within the United States. They enact and enforce laws and manage court systems, natural resources, and educational and other programs for their members. In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, many Native communities have sought to revitalize and reclaim their languages and cultures.

The NMAI discourages "Native" costumes and crafts in classrooms, because projects that copy Native traditions often perpetuate stereotypes. This lesson uses none. It uses precise names, present tense verbs and statements from government and museum sources.

How to run it

30 minutes, 5 steps. The time on the left is when each step starts.

  1. 0:00
    1

    Warm-up: count the tense

    4 min
    On the board

    Finish this sentence on paper with one verb: "Native Americans ___." Then circle whether your verb is in the past tense or the present tense.

    You do
    • Students write silently. Ask for a show of hands: past tense (such as lived or made) or present tense (such as live or make)?
    • Write the two counts on the board. Do not write or discuss the verbs themselves.

    Say: Hold on to these two numbers. We will use them again at the end.

  2. 4:00
    2

    The facts

    5 min
    On the board
    • 1990: November is first designated National American Indian Heritage Month. Congress chose November because it ended the traditional harvest season, a time of thanksgiving and celebration.
    • The United States recognizes more than 570 tribes and Alaska Native entities, each with its own history and traditions.
    • There is no single American Indian culture or language.
    • Tribal governments are sovereign nations within the United States. They enact and enforce laws and manage courts, natural resources and programs for their members.
    • Many Native communities are reviving and reclaiming their languages and cultures today.
    You do
    • Read the five facts aloud, one slide at a time.
    • After the third fact, ask: if someone says "Native Americans do this," what should we ask? (Which nation?)

    Say: Specific beats general. A nation's own name is more accurate than a label for hundreds of nations.

  3. 9:00
    3

    True or false, in the present tense

    9 min
    On the board

    True or false? 1. Native nations have their own governments today. 2. All Native Americans share one language and one culture. 3. Native communities are working to revive their languages. 4. The United States recognizes more than 570 tribes and Alaska Native entities. 5. Tribal governments make and enforce laws for their members. 6. Native American traditions are all the same.

    You do
    • Read each statement. Students vote with thumbs up for true and thumbs down for false.
    • After each vote, give the answer: 1 true, tribal governments operate as sovereign nations within the United States. 2 false, there is no single American Indian culture or language. 3 true, many communities are reviving and reclaiming their languages. 4 true, USA.gov lists 574 and the Bureau of Indian Affairs counted 575 in January 2026. 5 true, tribal governments enact and enforce laws. 6 false, Native traditions are distinct and specific to each nation.
    • When a statement is false, ask what would make it true.

    Say: A false statement is a good chance to ask: what would make it true?

  4. 18:00
    4

    Write it precisely

    8 min
    On the board

    Write two true sentences about Native nations today. Use a present tense verb. Name a nation if you know one, or write the question you would ask to find out. Starters: Tribal governments ___. Native communities are ___. Many nations ___.

    You do
    • Students write alone for four minutes, using only the facts on the board and what they already know.
    • Pairs swap and check each sentence for present tense and for any word that treats all Native people as one group.
    • Take three sentences aloud. If a student names a nation, check the name and the claim before it goes on the board.
    • Ask where students could find out what a nation says about itself. Good answers: the nation's own website or tribal government, a Native-led museum or a teacher-approved Native author.

    Say: A present tense verb and a nation's name tell the truth about people who are living today.

  5. 26:00
    5

    Exit ticket: redo the sentence

    4 min
    On the board

    Look at the two counts from the warm-up. Rewrite your first sentence with a present tense verb and, if you can, a nation's name.

    You do
    • Show the two counts from the warm-up again.
    • Students rewrite the sentence and hand it to you at the door. Compare how many are now in the present tense.

Discussion questions

Open an answer when you want it.

Why does the Smithsonian's teaching framework say there is no single American Indian culture or language?

In the Americas there are more than 2,000 tribal groups, and each has its own culture and way of life. Treating them as one hides that difference.

Why does it matter whether a sentence about Native people is in the past tense or the present tense?

A sentence only in the past tense can suggest that Native peoples no longer exist. Native nations have governments, languages and communities today, and many communities are reviving their languages.

What does it mean to say tribal governments are sovereign nations within the United States?

They govern their own affairs: they enact and enforce laws and manage courts, natural resources and programs for their members, and they deal with federal, state and local governments as governments. The NMAI calls this a government-to-government relationship.

Why does this lesson skip costumes, headdresses and craft projects?

The NMAI advises that crafts and costumes that copy Native traditions often perpetuate stereotypes. Learning from Native and museum sources about the present is more accurate.

Exit ticket

Rewrite your first sentence with a present tense verb. Name a nation if you can, or write the nation you want to learn about.

Any sentence in the present tense that does not treat all Native people as one group, such as "Native communities are reviving their languages." A student who cannot name a nation can write the question they would ask to find out.

Make it fit your class

Younger students

For kindergarten to grade 2, teach three ideas: there are many Native nations, they are here today, and each has its own name. Read aloud a picture book by a Native author from your library. The NMAI lists We Are Grateful: Otsaliheliga by Traci Sorell and Berry Song by Michaela Goade among its recommended titles about gratitude and seasonal harvests.

Older students

For grades 9 to 12, read the sections of the NMAI's Framework for Essential Understandings about American Indians on governance and sovereignty. Ask students to compare how the framework describes tribal governments with how their civics textbook describes federal and state governments.
