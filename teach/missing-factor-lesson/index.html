Grades 3 to 4

Multiplication and division

Missing factor lesson: the multiplication fact inside a division fact, in 25 minutes, straight from this page

A free missing factor lesson for grades 3 to 4: see why 24 ÷ 4 = ? asks the same question as 4 × ? = 24, build a fact family, and practice with a partner.

Grades
3 to 4, with changes for younger and older
Time
25 minutes
Prep
None. Project this page or read from it.
You need
Paper and pencil for each student. Counters for building arrays are optional.

What your class will see

Press Teach it now and the board plays these in order. Click any slide to start there.

What it is

A division fact can be solved as a missing factor problem. 24 ÷ 4 = ? asks the same question as 4 × ? = 24: what number multiplied by 4 makes 24? Common Core standard 3.OA.B.6 says it this way: "Understand division as an unknown-factor problem. For example, find 32 ÷ 8 by finding the number that makes 32 when multiplied by 8."

Standard 3.OA.A.4 asks students to "Determine the unknown whole number in a multiplication or division equation relating three whole numbers. For example, determine the unknown number that makes the equation true in each of the equations 8 × ? = 48, 5 = _ ÷ 3, 6 × 6 = ?." The sort in this lesson uses the first and last of those equation shapes, an unknown factor and an unknown product.

Students start with a muffin tray we made up, draw it as an array, name the three numbers in a fact family, check a missing factor with a multiple choice question and a true or false vote, sort equations by where the unknown is, and practice with a partner. Nothing is timed.

How to run it

25 minutes, 7 steps. The time on the left is when each step starts.

  1. 0:00
    1

    Warm-up: the muffin tray

    3 min
    On the board

    Imagine a muffin tray with 24 muffins in 4 equal rows. How many muffins are in each row?

    You do
    • Read the board aloud. Say that the tray is made up for this lesson.
    • Students think alone for one minute. They may draw or use counters.
    • Take two answers and the way each student found it. Do not say which is right yet.

    Say: Keep your answer. In a minute we will check it with a picture.

  2. 3:00
    2

    Draw the array

    4 min
    On the board

    Draw 24 dots in 4 equal rows. How many dots are in each row? How do you know the rows are equal?

    You do
    • Students draw four rows. They can place one dot in each row, then another in each row, until they have 24.
    • Show the drawn array on the slide, one row at a time.
    • Ask students to compare their picture with the slide and say what is the same.

    Say: Equal rows means every row has the same number.

  3. 7:00
    3

    The big idea and the fact family

    5 min
    On the board

    24 ÷ 4 = ? 4 × ? = 24 Three numbers: 24, 4 and 6. 4 × 6 = 24 6 × 4 = 24 24 ÷ 4 = 6 24 ÷ 6 = 4

    You do
    • Write the first two equations side by side. Ask what number makes both true.
    • Show the triangle with 24 on top and 4 and 6 below. Build the four equations one at a time.
    • Ask which numbers change places between the equations and which number stays on top.

    Say: Division is a missing factor question. 24 divided by 4 asks what times 4 makes 24.

  4. 12:00
    4

    Check and vote

    3 min
    On the board

    7 × ? = 56. What is the missing factor? True or false: 36 ÷ 9 = 4 is the same fact as 9 × 4 = 36.

    You do
    • Run the multiple choice question. Take a show of hands for each choice, then reveal the answer.
    • Run the vote. Ask one student to say how the three numbers appear in both equations.

    Say: Say the matching division sentence for 7 × 8 = 56.

  5. 15:00
    5

    Sort: where is the unknown?

    3 min
    On the board

    Sort each equation: FACTOR MISSING or PRODUCT MISSING ? × 6 = 42 8 × ? = 48 9 × ? = 36 ? × 5 = 30 6 × 6 = ? 7 × 4 = ? 3 × 8 = ? 9 × 2 = ?

    You do
    • Students sort the eight equations on the slide. They can drag them, or press Next and one equation sorts itself each time.
    • Point out that a division sentence can be read as a missing factor question.
  6. 18:00
    6

    Partner practice and strategies

    5 min
    On the board

    Partner A reads a missing factor card, for example ? × 6 = 42. Partner B says the matching division sentence. Then check it with multiplication and switch.

    You do
    • Write five missing factor equations on the board: ? × 6 = 42, 8 × ? = 56, ? × 9 = 63, 7 × ? = 35, ? × 4 = 32.
    • Partners take turns reading an equation and saying the matching division sentence. Each answer is checked with multiplication.
    • Spin the strategy wheel and let pairs try the strategy it lands on for 42 ÷ 6.

    Say: There is more than one good way to find a missing factor.

  7. 23:00
    7

    Exit ticket

    2 min
    On the board

    Draw an array. Write one multiplication sentence and one division sentence for it.

    You do
    • Students draw and write on a scrap of paper and hand it to you at the door.

Discussion questions

Open an answer when you want it.

How are 24 ÷ 4 = ? and 4 × ? = 24 the same question?

Both ask for the number that makes 24 when multiplied by 4. The answer is 6, so 24 ÷ 4 = 6 and 4 × 6 = 24.

Is 36 ÷ 9 = 4 the same fact as 9 × 4 = 36?

Yes. They use the same three numbers: 9 and 4 multiply to make 36, and 36 divided by 9 gives 4.

What is the difference between an unknown factor and an unknown product?

In 8 × ? = 48 the unknown is a factor, one of the numbers being multiplied. In 6 × 6 = ? the unknown is the product, the answer to the multiplication.

Which strategy would you use for 42 ÷ 6, and why?

Any strategy that works is fine: skip count by 6 to 42, use the known fact 6 × 7 = 42, draw an array, or write the fact family. A strong answer says why the strategy fits.

Exit ticket

Draw an array. Write one multiplication sentence and one division sentence for it.

Any array works if both sentences use its numbers. Example: 3 rows of 5 dots gives 3 × 5 = 15 and 15 ÷ 3 = 5.

Make it fit your class

Younger students

For grade 2 and early grade 3, stay with the arrays. Draw 12 dots in 3 rows and name the number in each row. Skip the fact family triangle and the sort.

Older students

For grades 5 and up, use larger numbers, such as ? × 12 = 96, and have students check each answer with the matching division sentence.
