A report card comment that families can use does three things: it names something the child does well and shows the evidence, it names one thing to work on in words a parent would use, and it gives a next step small enough to start this week. The three blogs we read give that formula three different names, and all three warn against the same failure, a comment a parent cannot act on.

We do not write report cards on this site, so nothing here is a story from a room. We read the blogs that teach report card comments, kept the names on their ideas, and wrote the part you can use directly: a formula to fill in and 80 original comments in five groups. Each group is in its own box and ready to copy. The blogs are credited by name, and the formula and the comments are ours.

The formula

Jessica Boschen at What I Have Learned writes it as "strength + evidence + next step" and aims for two to four sentences. Rebecca Davies at Differentiated Teaching opens with a strength, names a growth area and suggests a next step, and says the structure works only when each part is specific enough to act on. Jill and Cathy at The Curriculum Corner teach it as "2 strengths and a wish!": a positive, an area for improvement, and another strength to end on.

What the four pages actually say

These are their methods, in their words where we could keep them. The links go to the posts the ideas came from.

JB

Jessica Boschen

What I Have Learned

Writes the formula as "strength + evidence + next step" and keeps a comment to two to four sentences: one for a strength, one for progress or growth, and one suggestion if it is needed. Her bank is grouped by purpose: opening and closing comments, academic progress by subject, needs improvement, work habits and quality of work, classroom behavior, and social skills and character.

Her warnings are about words. "Needs to try harder" and "could do better" give a family nothing to change, so she names the skill and one next step instead, such as a practice, a routine, a strategy or support. For struggling students she pairs the concern with encouragement and a specific strategy, and she suggests student self-reflection sheets to set goals and give talking points for parent conferences.

RD

Rebecca Davies

Differentiated Teaching

Three tests for a comment: it is specific, naming the skill or behavior; honest, saying where the student really is; and it includes a concrete next step. A weak next step is "Continue to support your child at home." A stronger one is a defined task, such as 10 minutes of practice a night. Two to four sentences, unless the school sets a limit.

On tone, comments for kindergarten through grade 2 are more developmental and less evaluative, and upper elementary comments explain what a gap means for next year. On bad news she says to use clear language instead of vague reassurance: state plainly that the student is below grade-level expectations, explain what that means, and close with an invitation to a follow-up conversation.

JA

Jill and Cathy

The Curriculum Corner

Their formula is "2 strengths and a wish!": a positive, an area for improvement, and another strength to end on, the same pattern they teach students for peer feedback. For growth areas, begin with "working on," as in "working on improving responsibility" in place of "is not responsible."

They ask for honesty without harshness, and for something actionable instead of a label: rather than saying a child is below grade level, name the current reading level and suggest a step such as reading together for 10 minutes a night. They also say to comment only on skills you have actually taught, to reread for kindness because tone can be misread in writing, and to keep data on hand in student data binders and "I Can" checklists.

AM

Andrew Mangan

Understood, reviewed by Kate Garcia, MEd

Four tips for students with learning differences. Keep the student's individual goals in mind, and praise the process and progress instead of comparing to grade-level standards. Mention modifications. Talk with students before writing, or use reflection worksheets. And leave no surprises: share concerns and positives as they arise instead of waiting for the report card.

On accommodations, the article says that if a student's accommodations should change, that goes to the case manager and the IEP or 504 team, not onto the report card.

The phrases they cut

None of the four pages confesses a bad report card. Each one does name the lines that fail families, and the overlap is the useful part.

Jessica Boschen, What I Have Learned

"Needs to try harder" and "could do better." A family cannot tell what to change from either one. The replacement names the skill and gives one next step.

Rebecca Davies, Differentiated Teaching

Over-softening, such as "room to grow" for a student who is significantly behind. Generic praise that does not describe the student. Next steps nobody can act on. Jargon such as "phonemic awareness" or "self-regulation" with no plain-language explanation. And comparisons to other students.

Jill and Cathy, The Curriculum Corner

"Is not responsible," which becomes "working on improving responsibility." And a comment on a skill that was never taught, such as poor small-group work in a room where cooperative learning has not been built.

So the rule the three blogs agree on

A comment is finished when a parent could act on every sentence of it.

Praise with evidence, name one skill, give one next step, and cut anything a parent would have to ask you to explain.

Writing a set of comments, in four moves

Not a script. The order these pages imply, with the reason each piece sits where it does.

Gather one strength, one growth area, one next step per student

From your data and from the student. The Curriculum Corner keeps data in student binders and "I Can" checklists. Boschen and Understood both point to student reflection sheets.

Pick from the bank, then paste

Choose one or two strengths, one growth area or habit, and a next step. If a student is far behind, start from a specific positive that is true, then use the plain statement Davies describes.

Add a detail from your own observation

Boschen's rule for any bank. A work sample, a moment or a number all work. This is what turns a bank comment into a comment about this student.

Read it as the parent would

The Curriculum Corner says to reread for kindness, because tone can be misread in writing, and to proofread, because the report card also reflects on the teacher. Then check that nothing in it is news the family has not already heard. Understood says to share concerns as they arise.

What to skip

The all-purpose comment. Davies lists generic praise that does not describe the student. Some lines in the bank are broad on purpose, so they are starting points and not finished comments. If a comment could go on any student's report card, it needs its detail before it goes out.

Also skip the sandwich when the news is plain. "2 strengths and a wish" is a fine structure for most students, and Davies is just as clear that over-softening a real gap with a phrase like "room to grow" fails the family. Use the kind frame for most, and the plain statement for the student who is far behind.

Questions teachers ask

How long should a report card comment be?
Two to four sentences if your school sets no limit. Jessica Boschen gives one sentence to a strength, one to progress or growth, and one to a suggestion if it is needed. Rebecca Davies gives the same range as one specific strength, one growth area with a concrete example and one next step. If your report card system caps the length, the cap wins, and the formula still works at one sentence per part.
How do I write a comment for a student who is far behind?
Say it in clear language. Davies says to state plainly that the student is below grade-level expectations, explain what that means, and close with an invitation to a follow-up conversation, after opening with a specific positive that is true. The Curriculum Corner names the current level and a step, such as reading together for 10 minutes a night, instead of leaving the label alone.
Is it okay to use a comment bank?
Yes, as a starting point. Boschen's method is to swap in the student's name and add one specific detail you have observed. The bank on this page is written to be edited that way. Each group copies at once, then you change the brackets and add a detail from your own observation.
