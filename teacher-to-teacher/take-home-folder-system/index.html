What goes in a take-home folder, which side it goes on, and the routine that gets it back signed

A take-home folder holds three things and no more: work and flyers that stay home, anything needing a parent signature that comes back, and a reference section on prongs in the middle that never leaves the folder at all. The side a paper goes on is the whole system. Everything else is which folder you buy and what time of day you check it.

We do not run an elementary take-home folder in our own room, so this is not a diary. We read the pages that hold the first page for "what to put in a take home folder for parents," kept the teachers' names on their ideas, and lined the systems up next to each other, which turned out to be the useful part: they disagree about something basic, and nobody says so.

What goes in it

Five teachers, five folders, and a stable answer underneath the differences.

  • Stays homeGraded work, the weekly newsletter, school flyers, finished practice pages
  • Comes backPermission slips, signature forms, the behavior calendar or progress report, money envelopes
  • Never leavesReference pages on the prongs: multiplication chart, sight words, log-ins, the class schedule

The two sides, and the disagreement nobody mentions

Amy Murray at Teaching Exceptional Kinders labels her kindergarten pockets "LEFT at Home" and "Come RIGHT Back," which is the best mnemonic on any of these pages because a five year old can use it and so can a substitute.

Rebecca Rojas at Upper Elementary Snapshots does the exact opposite. On her folders the left side is the return side, carrying graded tests and the weekly progress report, and the right side is the keep-at-home side for routine classwork and flyers.

Both systems work. They cannot both be copied. Pick one convention on the day you print the labels, write it on the cover, and never change it mid-year, because the cost of the wrong side is not a lost paper, it is a parent who stops believing the folder means anything.

The five systems, credited

These are their folders, in their words where we could keep them. The links go to the posts the ideas came from.

AM

Amy Murray

Teaching Exceptional Kinders

Two-pocket poly folders with three prongs, a mailing label with the student's name on the front, and the two pockets marked "LEFT at Home" and "Come RIGHT Back." Reference material lives on the prongs so it cannot fall out.

Her collection routine is a turn-in tub on the back table or a named folder collector, and her line on why it holds is short: the more predictable the routine, the more sustainable it will be.

RR

Rebecca Rojas

Upper Elementary Snapshots

A weekly cycle rather than a daily one. Folders are loaded on Friday, go out Monday, and come back before the week ends. The return side carries graded tests, assignments and projects with a weekly progress report stapled to the top.

The parent signs the progress report, not every paper. That is the move that makes a weekly folder survive in a grade where there are thirty of them and a lot of paper in each.

EY

Emily Yerty

Polka Dots Please

The operational detail everyone else leaves out: stuff the folders before you hand them back at the end of the day, not in the morning. Loading a folder that is already in a backpack never happens.

Her morning routine is a basket by the door, one student who puts the folders in alphabetical order and names who is missing, and a teacher check during independent work rather than during arrival.

AW

Ashley Wright

The Wright Nook

Three-prong folders, fifty cents each at Target, with sheet protectors for the pages that stay. The newsletter is always the first page, every week, so a parent opening the folder always finds the same thing in the same place.

She reports one problem in over five years, and credits it to saying the expectation out loud at open house and again in the welcome packet rather than to the folder itself.

CC

Curious Classroom Adventures

Curious Classroom Adventures

A vinyl folder with brads for fourth grade, and page sleeve inserts in the middle holding the multiplication chart, spelling words, fraction strips and log-in information. The sleeves are what physically separate the return side from the stay-home side.

A homework tracker on top gets initialed daily by student and parent, which is the fourth-grade version of a signature line and takes about four seconds.

What they say goes wrong

The folder is never the failure. The failure is always in the routine around it, and four of these five say so directly.

Amy Murray

Sending home a folder with nothing in it is what kills the system. After two empty folders parents stop opening the third, and once they have stopped, a signature form put in the folder is a signature form that is not coming back.

She keeps the paper colors consistent for the same reason: a parent should recognize what a page is before reading it.

Curious Classroom Adventures

Some parents will not read your emails. That is her stated reason for printing the newsletter even though it also goes out digitally, and it is why the folder exists at all in a school that already has an app.

Emily Yerty

Folders loaded in the morning do not get loaded. Her whole routine is built to move the loading to the end of the previous day, when the papers are already in your hand.

So the rule the five of them agree on

A take-home folder is a routine with a folder in it, not a folder with a routine around it.

Two sides that never swap, a reference section that never leaves, and a fixed minute of the day when an adult who is not you does the checking.

Setting up a full class in one afternoon

Four steps, in this order, because each one removes work from the next.

Buy one folder type and print one cover

Three-prong two-pocket folders are what four of the five use. Print a cover with the student's name and, on the cover itself, the sentence that says which side does what. The cover is where a substitute and a parent both look first.

Label the pockets before you fill them

Amy Murray's "LEFT at Home" and "Come RIGHT Back" or Rebecca Rojas's return-on-the-left. Either one, printed, on every folder, identical. Handwriting the labels is how the convention drifts by November.

Load the prongs once, in August

Class schedule, log-ins, the reference charts your grade actually uses, and the parent letter that explains the folder. Sheet protectors on the pages that will be handled all year.

Assign the morning job before the first folder comes back

Basket by the door, one student sorting and naming who is missing, teacher check during independent work. Teach it in week one while there is nothing in the folders worth losing.

What to skip

The daily signature on every paper. Rebecca Rojas replaced it with one weekly progress report signed once, and that is the version that survives thirty students. A parent asked to sign eleven things signs none of them by week three.

Skip the second folder too. A reading folder, a homework folder and a communication folder means three things to remember and three things to lose. Every page above runs one folder with sections in it.

Questions teachers ask

What should go in a take-home folder?
A left side and a right side, one that stays home and one that comes back, plus a reference section on prongs in the middle that never leaves. Graded work and flyers go on the stay-home side, anything needing a signature goes on the come-back side. Students learn it in a week because the rule never changes.
How do I get folders back signed?
Check them at the same point every morning as part of the entry routine rather than asking for them. Emily Yerty puts a basket by the door and gives one student the job of sorting them and naming who is missing, which turns a reminder into a routine.
Do take-home folders work in upper elementary?
Yes, but the student owns the routine instead of the teacher. Rebecca Rojas runs a weekly version in upper elementary where a progress report is stapled to the top of the return side and the parent signs that rather than each paper.
